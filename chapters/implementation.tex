This chapter describes the software and hardware implementation of the system designed in Chapter~\ref{chap:design}.

\section{Data Acquisition Campaign}
\label{sec:data_acquisition}

The development and training of the machine learning backend required the construction of a robust ground-truth dataset spanning diverse ergonomic and biomechanical profiles. Data was collected from 69 participants via two separate campaigns: a controlled task dataset in-house, including both gamers and non-gamers, and a free-play dataset at the PolyLAN event. The two datasets were collected under different conditions to capture both structured and unstructured click dynamics.

\subsection{Controlled Task Dataset (BIC Event)}
The first deployment cohort ($N=26$) comprised a mix of competitive gamers to casual novices playing \textit{Counter-Strike 2} (CS2). The protocol ran for approximately 40 minutes per user and was strictly structured to isolate specific click dynamics:

\paragraph{Configuration A/B testing.} Following a uniform warmup phase, users played two 15-minute game blocks. The device hardware profile was hot-swapped halfway through the session, alternating between an aggressive configuration (AP at 1, RT at 1) and a highly conservative configuration (AP at 5, zero Rapid Trigger). The presentation order was randomized to mitigate sequence bias.

\paragraph{Weapon rotations.} Within each 15-minute block, users were instructed to rotate their active weapon every five minutes. The arsenal was specifically chosen to evoke distinct motor patterns: the \textit{USP-S} pistol for rapid individual tapping, the \textit{M4A1-S} rifle for sustained tracking and spraying, and the \textit{AWP} sniper rifle for slow, deliberate holding and release.

\subsection{Free-Play Dataset (PolyLAN Event)}
The secondary dataset ($N=43$) focused on unstructured free-play in a high-density LAN environment. Participants engaged in approximately 40-minute play sessions in their preferred title (\textit{CS2}, \textit{Valorant}, or \textit{League of Legends}). Similar to the controlled dataset, the hardware configurations were randomly (but uniformly) swapped from conservative to aggressive baseline profiles (and vice-versa) near the 20-minute mark to capture a wider range of organic responses. FPS players produced predominantly left-button click profiles consistent with primary-fire mechanics; \textit{League of Legends} players generated a contrasting signature of high-frequency right-click interactions, characteristic of point-and-click action commands.

The aggregated dataset generated from these two campaigns ultimately produced tens of hours of raw 1\,kHz telemetry across 69 participants, demanding the variance-based activity trimming described later in this chapter to isolate periods of genuine engagement before entering the classification pipelines.

\subsection{Annotated Dataset Summary}
Following manual annotation and variance-based trimming, the final curated dataset comprises 132 labeled recording files from 39 FPS-player participants, totaling approximately 12,556 seconds (\textasciitilde3.5 hours) of verified active telemetry. Left-channel annotations account for 39,088 labeled click cycles and right-channel annotations for 6,645 cycles, reflecting the predominance of primary-fire left-button interactions in FPS gameplay. The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test splits performed at the participant level to enforce strict subject isolation. We decided to hold out the League of Legends participants from the scope of this thesis, as their click dynamics were sufficiently distinct from the FPS cohort to warrant a separate study. The final dataset used for model training and evaluation consists of a total of 39 participants, using a split of 80\,\% training, 10\,\% validation, and 10\,\% test. The participant-level split ensures that no individual appears in more than one partition, preventing data leakage and ensuring that model performance reflects generalization to unseen users.

\section{Frontend and Telemetry Stack}
The user-facing layer provides the orchestration dashboard, recording states, and test minigames. Telemetry ingestion is handled out-of-process by a dedicated background sensor service, ensuring that 1\,kHz hardware polling never stalls the frontend workflows.

\subsection{Analog Decoder and Translator}
Raw telemetry is stored as a sparse binary event stream capturing only state-change transitions. A dedicated decoder parses this format and resamples the event sequence to a uniform 1\,ms grid using Modified Akima (MAKIMA) interpolation, which preserves the monotonic shape of the analog depth signal without the overshoot characteristic of standard cubic splines. Short resting gaps receive synthetic zero-value anchors before interpolation to prevent overshoot across idle periods. The output per frame consists of the quantized analog depth on a 0--120 scale, a digital press bit for each button, and a wall-clock timestamp. A client-side signal processing layer subsequently derives the kinematic channels---velocity, acceleration, jerk, and resting drift---from the interpolated depth, making them available for visualization and downstream annotation.

\subsection{Labeling Interface}
\label{sec:labeling_impl}
The annotation interface renders the decoded 1\,kHz analog depth signal for both buttons alongside the derived kinematic channels, enabling annotators to judge biomechanical intent from the full kinematic context rather than raw depth alone. MAKIMA interpolation is applied at twice the native sampling rate on the client side to smooth the velocity overlay for visual clarity, without introducing causal shift. Annotators interact with segment boundaries via draggable handles: the start and end boundaries of each labeled region can be adjusted independently per channel, whole segments can be repositioned by body-drag, and new segments are created by dragging in unlabeled space. Visual overlays distinguish manual annotations from algorithmically generated candidates, and highlight model-generated suggestions alongside their associated confidence scores.

Annotation followed the three-phase iterative strategy described in Section~\ref{sec:labeling}. The bootstrapping heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:labeling}) is implemented as a pure TypeScript module shared between the interactive labeling UI and an offline batch labeler, so both paths produce identical candidates from identical inputs. It operates on Savitzky-Golay-smoothed depth (order-2, odd window sizes from a precomputed coefficient table) rather than raw samples, since the raw 120-level stream is too noisy for a naive extremum search to be robust.

The Make boundary is found by a running-minimum search with patience: walking backward from the click's reported start, the algorithm tracks the lowest depth seen so far and stops once that minimum has not improved for 15 consecutive frames (or after a 200-frame search cap), returning the frame at which the minimum occurred. This is deliberately more conservative than a first-local-minimum search, since isolated micro-tremor troughs would otherwise terminate the walk prematurely. The Break boundary is found by walking forward from the same start point over the smoothed depth's finite-difference speed, returning the first frame where speed drops below $-0.05$ (in normalized depth units per ms); this hysteresis margin, rather than a literal zero-crossing, absorbs sensor noise near the peak. If no such frame is found before the click's reported end, the search falls back to that end index rather than leaving the boundary undefined. Both thresholds, along with the search window and patience length, are exposed as user-tunable parameters persisted across sessions, since their optimal values were found to vary slightly between annotators' preferred visualization settings.

This heuristic performs well on clean, well-separated clicks but has systematic failure modes that motivate the full annotation pipeline. It relies entirely on a detected digital event as an anchor, so clicks that were biomechanically executed but not registered by the firmware---because the actuation threshold was set deeper than the finger reached---are entirely invisible to it. The Make search remains sensitive to sustained noise or drift that shifts the running minimum away from the true rest position, while the Break search can be fooled by a slow, low-speed release that never crosses the hysteresis margin, silently falling back to the reported end frame. A separate post-hoc pass detects and trims boundary collisions when adjacent detected clicks overlap. These failure modes---unregistered presses, drift-induced misplacements, and slow releases---are precisely the edge cases most informative for training a robust intent model, and are the primary reason human review remained indispensable throughout the annotation process.

\section{Machine Learning Backend}
\label{sec:ml_backend}

The machine learning pipeline is encapsulated in a dedicated Python intent-detection backend built with scikit-learn, LightGBM~\cite{ke_lightgbm_2017}, and PyTorch.

\subsection{Loading and Frame Extraction}
Each recording file is paired with a JSON label sidecar validated at load time. The 120-level analog depth stream is normalised to $[0,1]$ by dividing by 120, and binary digital press signals are cast to \{0.0, 1.0\}. Each manually annotated click segment maps to three frame-level classes: Make Onset, Click Hold, and Break Onset. Onset zones extend $\pm 4$\,ms from each boundary marker to absorb natural annotation imprecision---a tolerance calibrated empirically to preserve signal sharpness while remaining robust to small annotator variability---and are clamped against adjacent segment boundaries to prevent cross-segment label contamination. A mtime-keyed in-memory cache prevents redundant re-parsing of unchanged files.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Signal Augmentation}
The analog channels optionally receive temporal smoothing (moving average, Gaussian, Savitzky-Golay, median, or EMA). Kinematic derivative channels---velocity, acceleration, jerk, and their absolute-value variants---are computed via zero-phase Akima-spline upsampling followed by centered finite differences and downsampling, eliminating causal shift. A resting-drift channel is derived as the difference of a fast and a slow exponential moving average, both applied zero-phase, capturing low-frequency postural drift independently of click dynamics. Continuous channels are optionally decimated using a zero-phase Chebyshev IIR low-pass filter before striding; discrete digital channels are strided only. A rolling-window activity mask based on configurable amplitude and variance thresholds trims inactive interior gaps, retaining short margins around each boundary and all labeled frames, and splits the recording into independent active sub-sequences.

\subsection{Splitting, Normalization, and Sample Weighting}
Sub-sequences are partitioned into train, validation, and test at the sequence level. The preferred strategy is participant-level k-fold cross-validation: sequences are grouped by \texttt{user\_id} (extracted from the file path via \texttt{p\textbackslash d+} regex), each fold holds out exactly one participant group. Z-score normalization is fitted on the training partition only in order to prevent information leakage from the validation and test sets. For kinematic channels (velocity, acceleration, jerk, drift) the standard deviation is computed over non-background frames exclusively to prevent the background mass from compressing the feature scale. Binary digital and constant per-recording configuration channels are excluded from normalization.

Click-balanced per-frame sample weights equalize participant contributions. For participant $u$ with $C_u$ click events and $b_u$ background frames, the weight of a frame belonging to click event $e$ spanning $|F_e|$ frames is $(1/C_u)/|F_e|$, and the weight of a background frame is $1/b_u$. Weights are then rescaled per class to preserve the click/background mass ratio. The Random Forest additionally applies inverse-frequency class weights scaled by configurable per-class multipliers, providing an independent knob for the cost of each misclassification type, though this did not yield significant performance gains in practice.

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Model Ingestion}
For the Random Forest and Gradient Boosted Trees, sliding-window feature extraction produces one tabular vector per frame. Each window is centered on the target frame and computes a fixed bank of per-channel statistics: window mean, first-to-last slope, peak, velocity at the centre frame, time-to-peak, minimum, and time-to-trough. A set of cross-channel features is always appended: left/right analog correlation, whether each digital channel fires during an analog rise, and the maximum analog value across both switches. The feature set grows by one additional per-channel block for each derived channel enabled (e.g.\ velocity, acceleration, jerk, drift). An extended feature bank is also available, adding higher-order statistics such as zero-crossing rate, TKEO energy, and kurtosis. Optional context probes append raw or locally averaged analog samples at configurable temporal offsets (e.g.\ $\pm$50\,ms, $\pm$100\,ms).

The FCN bypasses tabular extraction and receives raw $[C, T]$ tensors directly. The architecture consists of an input projection convolution, eight stacked dilated residual blocks each containing two convolutional layers, and a per-frame output classifier---18 convolutional layers in total. The dilation factor doubles at each successive block ($1, 2, 4, \ldots, 128$), producing an effective receptive field of
\[
  RF = 2(k-1)(2^D - 1)
\]
which evaluates to 3\,060 frames ($\approx$3\,s at 1\,kHz) with kernel size $k = 7$ and $D = 8$ dilation stages. Each block applies symmetric non-causal padding---equal context on both sides of each convolution---so the model introduces no temporal shift in its predictions. A ReLU activation and dropout are applied between the two convolutional layers of each block; the skip connection within each residual block is an identity map throughout, as all blocks share the same hidden channel dimension. Training labels are converted to a 3-channel heatmap: channels 0 and 1 carry Gaussian probability bumps ($\sigma$ = 8 frames) centred on each Make and Break onset frame respectively, and channel 2 carries a binary hold mask. Because onset frames are vastly outnumbered by non-onset frames within each channel---even after the click-balanced weighting of Section~\ref{sec:ml_backend} equalizes participant and event contributions, the great majority of frames in any window are still confidently-negative background---standard binary cross-entropy would let these easy negatives dominate the gradient. We therefore use the focal loss of Lin et al.~\cite{lin_focal_2017}, which multiplies the per-frame BCE term by a modulating factor that vanishes as the model's confidence on that frame increases:
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}(p, y) &= (1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \cdot \text{BCE}(p, y), \\
  p_t &= y p + (1-y)(1-p)
\end{align*}
where $p$ is the sigmoid output, $y \in [0,1]$ the heatmap target, and $\gamma$ the focusing parameter ($\gamma = 2$, the default proposed by Lin et al.). As training progresses and background frames are classified with increasing confidence ($p_t \to 1$), their contribution to the loss is suppressed, concentrating gradient signal on the sparse, still-ambiguous onset frames. The loss is computed per channel per frame without reduction, then scaled by the per-frame sample weights from Section~\ref{sec:ml_backend} before averaging, so participant- and event-balancing and difficulty-based re-weighting act jointly rather than one overriding the other. When sequence-window training is used, an 8-frame zero-weight gap is inserted between recordings in the concatenated hypersequence so no sliding window can span a boundary.

\subsection{Gradient Boosted Trees}
The Gradient Boosted Trees model shares the sliding-window tabular feature extraction pipeline described above; every per-frame feature vector is identical to the one supplied to the Random Forest. The distinguishing design concerns are the training objective and the label targets.

The pipeline additionally supports replacing hard one-hot labels at onset boundaries with asymmetric soft targets: a split-normal Gaussian centred on each annotated onset frame, with independent standard deviations on either side---for Make Onset, a wider left than right standard deviation encodes that detecting slightly early is less harmful than a late detection, and for Break Onset the asymmetry is reversed. This soft-labeling path is implemented but was not used for the reported GBT results; the models evaluated in Chapter~\ref{chap:eval} are trained on standard hard labels.

For the reported results, training uses the multiclass generalization of focal loss,
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}(p_c) = -(1 - p_c)^{\gamma}\log p_c,
\]
where $c$ is the ground-truth class (Make Onset, Break Onset, Hold, or Background) and $p_c$ the softmax probability the model assigns to it, for the same reason motivating its use in the FCN: background frames vastly outnumber onset frames, and standard cross-entropy would let the many easy, confidently-classified background frames dominate the boosting gradient at the expense of the rare onset transitions. LightGBM does not expose focal loss natively, so it is supplied as a custom objective: the standard softmax cross-entropy gradient and Hessian are each scaled by a per-sample focal factor derived from $p_c$ and $\gamma$, which attenuates both to near zero for confidently-correct frames and leaves them close to their unscaled values for hard or misclassified frames, concentrating tree splits on the latter. Sample weights are folded into this same custom objective, since LightGBM's built-in dataset weights are not applied to gradients supplied by a custom objective function. Early stopping is evaluated on hard-label accuracy over the held-out validation partition. When the soft-label path is enabled instead, the one-hot target in the cross-entropy term is replaced by the split-normal soft targets described above, yielding a custom gradient and Hessian that align the objective with the continuous target distribution rather than a hard class assignment; this configuration was not exercised for the results in this thesis.

\section{From Classification to Segmentation}
\label{sec:segmentation_impl}

The per-frame classifier produces a probability array over all four classes for the full recording. Converting these continuous outputs into discrete (start, end) click intervals requires three sequential stages: identifying candidate onset regions, electing a single representative frame from each region, and pairing each Make onset with a corresponding Break onset.

Segmentation is implemented as a single module shared, unmodified, between the offline training/evaluation pipeline and the online labeling daemon, with parity between the two paths enforced by dedicated regression tests; this guarantees that the strategy selected during validation behaves identically at inference time.

\subsection{Candidate Run Detection}
Make and Break run boundaries are located with a vectorized scan---computing the forward difference of a boolean above-threshold mask---rather than a per-frame loop, which replaced an earlier $O(T)$ while-loop implementation in the daemon with an equivalent scan over run boundaries only. The Make channel additionally applies a Schmitt-trigger hysteresis, opening a run when probability exceeds the entry threshold and closing it only once probability falls to half that value, which prevents a single physical press from fragmenting into multiple candidates when its probability hump dips transiently due to softmax saturation or quantization noise. Break runs use a single-threshold scheme, as the Break probability signal is generally sharper and less prone to mid-run fluctuations. Because the same make- and break-run scan is repeated at every point of the hyperparameter sweep described below, per-run onset frames are precomputed once per election strategy and cached, so re-evaluating a new threshold pair against an already-computed set of runs does not repeat the election computation.

\subsection{Onset Frame Election}
Three election strategies are implemented: \textbf{peak} (argmax over the run), \textbf{plateau median} (median index among frames within 0.5\,\% of the run peak, falling back to the peak itself when the plateau collapses to a single frame), and \textbf{steepest rise/fall} (the frame of maximum probability gradient, with index 0 explicitly excluded from the search to avoid a finite-difference boundary artifact, and a fallback to the run's first frame when the curve never falls). All three run in linear time over the number of candidate runs, and are vectorized to avoid per-run loops. The steepest-rise/fall strategy is the most robust to plateauing, but is sensitive to noise in the probability gradient; the plateau-median strategy is more robust to noise but can be biased toward the center of a long plateau; and the peak strategy is the simplest and fastest, but can be misled by spurious local maxima. The optimal strategy is selected via hyperparameter sweep on the held-out validation set.

\subsection{Make--Break Pairing}
Pairing is posed as an assignment problem over a dense $N_m \times N_b$ cost matrix, solved exactly via \texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}; invalid (Make, Break) combinations are excluded from consideration with a large sentinel cost. This Hungarian-assignment path costs $O(N_m \times N_b)$ to build and is cubic to solve, which motivates keeping a \textbf{greedy first-fit} alternative---pairing each Make with the first unclaimed eligible Break via a monotonically advancing pointer, $O(N_m + N_b)$---for the largest concatenated evaluation sets. A \textbf{trained pairing scorer} (logistic regression over standardized features, fit with balanced class weights) replaces the raw confidence product as the assignment cost. Its six features are Make confidence, Break confidence, log inter-onset duration, Make run width, Break run width, and the number of competing Break runs within the Make's search window. Critically, the scorer is trained only on \emph{ambiguous} Makes---those with two or more competing Break candidates---since unambiguous cases carry no information about how to disambiguate. The reported training-set precision/recall for the scorer is therefore optimistic by construction and is not used to select the deployed strategy---instead, all three pairing strategies are compared against each other on held-out event-level F1, and the winner (with IoU as a tie-breaker) is the one stored and shipped.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection via Bayesian Sweep}
The optimal combination of election strategy, entry threshold, and exit threshold is determined by maximizing the segmentation IoU between predicted and ground-truth click intervals on the held-out validation set. A Bayesian search using a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler~\cite{bergstra_tpe_2011} is employed via Optuna~\cite{optuna_akiba_2019}, backed by a persistent SQLite store that allows sweeps to be paused and resumed across sessions. When the Cartesian product of the discrete search space falls at or below 200 candidate combinations, the sampler automatically transitions to exhaustive grid enumeration to guarantee full coverage. The elected strategy, pairing strategy, and threshold values are stored alongside the trained model weights so that inference uses the identical configuration selected during validation.

\section{Optimization Pipeline Integration}
\label{sec:optimizer_impl}

The parameter optimizer is implemented as a standalone Rust service that communicates with the front-end client via named-pipe IPC. It receives preprocessed analog signals and labeled click regions from the ML manager, performs an exhaustive discrete search over candidate thresholds, and returns both best-solution and analysis artifacts as JSON outputs.

The core of the service is a frame-accurate simulation of firmware trigger logic, illustrated in Figures~\ref{fig:fsm_nort} and~\ref{fig:fsm_rt}.

\input{chapters/figures/fig-fsm-rt}

The deployed optimization path uses depth for both make and break channels, with candidate thresholds $(T_m, T_r)$ where $T_m$ is Actuation Point and $T_r$ is Rapid Trigger sensitivity. In this path, the simulator runs a 3-state FSM (Idle $\rightarrow$ Pressed $\rightarrow$ RT-Released):

\begin{itemize}
  \item Idle $\rightarrow$ Pressed if $m_i \ge T_m$ (emit make, initialize running peak),
  \item Pressed $\rightarrow$ RT-Released if $b_i \le \mathrm{peak} - T_r$ (emit break, initialize running valley),
  \item RT-Released $\rightarrow$ Pressed if $b_i \ge \mathrm{valley} + T_r$ (emit make),
  \item RT-Released $\rightarrow$ Idle only when the signal returns to zero ($b_i \le 0$ or $\mathrm{valley} \le 0$), matching firmware reset behavior.
\end{itemize}

For non-depth break features, the backend also supports a simple 2-state threshold mode with candidate $(T_m, T_b)$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item unpressed $\rightarrow$ pressed when $m_i \ge T_m$,
  \item pressed $\rightarrow$ unpressed when $b_i \le T_b$.
\end{itemize}

Signals are quantized before evaluation so search remains firmware-faithful and efficient. In the deployed depth-depth path, thresholds are evaluated exhaustively over the exposed 10-level depth grid.

For each threshold candidate, simulated intervals are aligned to labels using overlap-constrained monotonic matching. The service computes timing and detection metrics (matched, missed, false positives, make-early, break-early, and latency summaries), evaluates the objective described in Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}, and stores per-candidate simulated intervals and aggregate statistics.

Output artifacts include the complete set of evaluated candidates, the minimum-loss solution, a Pareto frontier over \{error rate, average click distance\}, and landscape data for visualization.
