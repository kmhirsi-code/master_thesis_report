\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:eval}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This chapter evaluates the system introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}
along two complementary axes. Sections~\ref{sec:eval-setup}--\ref{sec:eval-opt}
evaluate the offline pipeline in isolation: the frame-level click-intent classifier
and its classification-to-segmentation pipeline (Sections~\ref{sec:eval-results}
and~\ref{sec:eval-seg}), and the firmware threshold optimizer built on top of it
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt}), each against an appropriate baseline on held-out or
full-corpus data. Section~\ref{sec:eval-user-study} then closes the loop with an
end-to-end user study that tests whether the optimizer's theoretical gains translate
into measurable gameplay performance for real participants, alongside a separate,
corpus-wide measurement of participants' subjective trust in the suggested
configuration.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Dataset and Splits}
All experiments use the curated corpus described in Section~\ref{sec:data_acquisition}:
132 annotated recordings from 39 participants, yielding 12,556,288 frames
at 1\,kHz.  Separate models are trained for the left (L) and right (R) button channels,
reflecting the large asymmetry in click volume (39,088 left-button clicks versus 6,645
right-button clicks).  The data is split at the participant level into
training (10,794,087 frames), validation (789,538 frames), and held-out test
(972,663 frames) partitions; the same split is reused for both channel models, since
the L- and R-channel labels are derived from the same underlying telemetry frames.
No participant's recordings appear in more than one
partition.  Generalization is assessed with 5-fold grouped cross-validation, where each
fold holds out a distinct group of participants (leave-one-participant-group-out), on
the training and validation pool; the test set is evaluated exactly once per model
after all hyperparameter selection is complete.

\subsection{Baselines}

\paragraph{Heuristic bootstrap.} The OS-event-anchored walk-back detector described
in Section~\ref{sec:labeling}, evaluated as a segmentor on the test set.
Represents the pre-model ceiling achievable without a learned component.

\paragraph{Static default firmware configuration.} Actuation Point at level 5/10
(mid-travel), Rapid Trigger disabled. Serves as the optimizer comparison point.

\subsection{Metrics}
\paragraph{Detection and segmentation.}
A predicted click is counted as a true positive (TP) only if \emph{both} its
predicted make-onset and break-onset fall within a fixed tolerance
$r=\text{\texttt{DEPLOYMENT\_EVAL\_RADIUS\_MS}}=4$\,ms of the corresponding
ground-truth boundary centers (the midpoints of the labeled make and break
onset windows). Matching is one-to-one and greedy, performed via binary search
over ground-truth make-onset centers. A predicted click that satisfies the
make-onset radius but misses the break-onset radius (or vice versa) is
\emph{not} a TP: it is counted as both a false positive (the unmatched
prediction) and a false negative (the unmatched ground-truth click). This
dual-radius criterion is deliberately stricter than a single overlap or IoU
threshold, since a click is only useful downstream if both of its boundaries
are individually accurate. \emph{Deployment F1} is the harmonic mean of
click-level precision and recall under this criterion. \emph{Mean IoU} is a
separate, purely diagnostic Jaccard index computed only over matched (TP)
pairs, and says nothing about clicks that failed the dual-radius gate; this is
why IoU can remain high even when precision and recall are low
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion}).

\paragraph{Boundary timing.}
For each matched pair we compute the signed make-boundary error $e^{(m)}$ and
break-boundary error $e^{(b)}$ in milliseconds (positive = late, negative = early).
We report the mean bias and the absolute spread (mean absolute deviation) per boundary.
Boundary timing details are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:latency}.

\input{chapters/figures/fig-latency}

\paragraph{Cross-validation.}
For all three architectures (Random Forest, GBT, and FCN), 5-fold grouped
cross-validation deployment F1 is reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation, in
addition to single held-out test set performance.

\paragraph{Optimizer objective and metrics.}
The firmware threshold optimizer (Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt}) scores each
candidate (AP, RT) configuration with the loss function derived in
Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}, $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{TP}\sum_i d_i +
\frac{c_\text{miss} FN + c_\text{fp} FP}{N}$, whose components map onto the
four metrics reported below: \emph{TP rate}, the fraction of labeled click
intents the configuration successfully registers under the FSM simulator;
\emph{click distance}, the per-match timing term $d_i$ averaged over matched
clicks; and \emph{make latency} and \emph{break latency}, the signed timing
offsets in milliseconds between the simulated make/break firmware event and
the corresponding labeled boundary. All four objective parameters were set
once by manual tuning against pilot sessions, rather than a formal
sensitivity sweep, and then held fixed across all reported sessions
($w_\text{make}=0.6$, $c_\text{det}=40$\,ms, $\beta=0$, $\kappa=2.0$, in
Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}'s notation) so that configurations are compared
on a common scale.
Statistical consistency of the optimizer's improvement over the static
default across sessions is assessed
with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the per-session loss delta. The Wilcoxon
test is chosen over a paired $t$-test because it makes no assumption of
normally distributed deltas and is robust to the outlier sessions where the
static default fails catastrophically (very large loss); it evaluates the
null hypothesis that the paired loss differences are symmetric around zero,
against the one-sided alternative that the optimized configuration's loss is
stochastically smaller.

\paragraph{User-study statistics.}
The end-to-end user study (Section~\ref{sec:eval-user-study}) reports, per
task and metric: the mean $\Delta$ (change from calibration to test round) for
each group, a two-sided Welch's $t$-test (unequal variances) between the
experimental and placebo groups' $\Delta$ values ($p$) at $\alpha=0.05$, and
the standardized effect size (Cohen's $d$)
with its 95\,\% confidence interval, computed via
$SE(d) = \sqrt{(n_1+n_2)/(n_1 n_2) + d^2/(2(n_1+n_2-2))}$ for independent
groups of size $n_1, n_2$. Required sample size for a target power of 0.80 at
the observed effect sizes is estimated with Lehr's approximation
($n \approx 16/d^2$ per group), noting that sample-size estimates derived from
an observed effect size in an underpowered study tend to be optimistic, since
significant effects from small samples are systematically inflated.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All timings are measured on an AMD Ryzen~9 7900X (12-core, 4.7\,GHz), 16\,GB RAM,
with an NVIDIA RTX~5080 GPU.  The Random Forest and GBT train on CPU; the FCN uses
PyTorch with CUDA acceleration for both training and inference.

All models achieve sub-2\,ms mean boundary bias, well within the $\pm4$\,ms
matching radius used to score correctness --- this reflects tight agreement
with the ground-truth boundary annotation itself, rather than an
independently established perceptual threshold for click timing.
The heuristic bootstrap achieves
a pooled deployment F1 of only 19.9\,\% on the left channel across the full corpus,
with per-participant F1 ranging from 0.1\,\% to 75.0\,\%; the right channel
is pooled at 12.2\,\% with an even wider per-participant range of 0.0\,\% to 43.5\,\%

The dual-radius matching criterion (Section~\ref{sec:eval-setup}) means a click is
only a true positive if both its make- and break-onsets are individually accurate;
the heuristic's duration estimate can still be accurate when a click is matched at
all (IoU~91.6\,\%), but that says nothing about the many clicks it fails to match
under the stricter dual-radius gate (see Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion} for why
this matched-pair IoU is comparable to the RF's despite the large F1 gap).  This gap
--- 19.9\,\% vs.\ 97.0\,\% for the RF --- directly quantifies the value of the
learned pipeline.
The FCN underperforms significantly
on the R channel (F1 83.6\,\% vs.\ 93.6\,\% for L), attributable to the much smaller

Figure~\ref{fig:gen-gap} quantifies the corresponding accuracy-based

Figure~\ref{fig:fcn-data-ablation} plots the same three metrics against
training-corpus size on a log axis, making the contrast between channels
visually explicit: the left-channel curves separate cleanly and trend upward,
while the right-channel curves are non-monotonic throughout. On the left
channel, deployment F1 rises from 79.13\,\% at the smallest corpus tested
(499,037 training frames, 119 ground-truth clicks in the held-out evaluation
split) to 94.53\,\% at the largest (10,550,000 frames, 2,230 clicks) --- within
2.5 points of the RF's 97.0\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:deployment}), though this
comparison spans different evaluation splits (this run's 672,590-frame split
vs.\ the RF's 972,663-frame split) and so is only approximate, despite an
architecture and training regime
Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion} otherwise finds harder to justify. The gain
is front-loaded: the first corpus increase (499,037$\to$993,667 frames) buys
12.03 points of F1, while the last (5,207,106$\to$10,550,000 frames) buys only
0.70, consistent with the left-channel FCN approaching a data-scaling plateau
near the current corpus size, in line with its near-parity IoU against the RF
already noted in Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion}.

The right channel tells a different story. Deployment F1 is non-monotonic
across all five scales tested (75.00\,\%, 69.23\,\%, 71.47\,\%, 62.34\,\%,
81.26\,\%), dipping to its lowest value at the second-largest corpus
(893,240 frames) before recovering to its best value at the largest
(1,883,715 frames). With only 29--518 ground-truth clicks per evaluation split,
each run's deployment F1 is a click-level statistic over a small sample, so
a handful of missed or spurious detections shifts the pooled score by several
points --- the ablation is too noisy at this scale to establish a clean
trend the way the left channel's is. The best right-channel result (81.26\,\%
at 1,883,715 frames) is at least consistent with more data helping, though the
non-monotonic pattern above means this single point should not be read as
confirming a trend: it is 6.26 points above the next-best right-channel run
and 9.90 points below the nearest-in-size left-channel run
(91.16\,\% at 993,667 frames, itself $1.90\times$ smaller). The largest
right-channel corpus tested here is still $5.6\times$ smaller than the
left-channel's largest, and smaller than every left-channel point beyond the
second, so this ablation cannot yet distinguish between two explanations:
(a)~the right channel's signal is inherently harder for the FCN to exploit at
any practical scale, or (b)~the right channel simply has not yet reached the
corpus size or sample count at which the left channel's gains stabilize.
Growing the right-channel corpus toward the $\sim$5.2\,million-frame range where
the left channel's improvement rate first slows is the natural next
experiment to disambiguate these.

Training cost scales accordingly. Left-channel FCN training time grows from 226\,s at
the smallest corpus scale to 2,695\,s (44.9\,min) at the largest, an $11.9\times$
increase for a $21.1\times$ increase in training-corpus size --- sublinear because
larger corpora need fewer epochs to reach the early-stopping criterion (31 epochs at
the smallest L scale vs.\ 17 at the largest; 60 vs.\ 16 for R). Closing the
right-channel data gap identified above would therefore push FCN-R training time
toward the same regime already flagged as impractical for on-demand retraining in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion}. Feature importance is comparatively stable across
scale: \texttt{accel} remains the largest or second-largest contributor at every
corpus size and in both channels (23--37\,\% of total importance), so the data-scaling
behavior above reflects how much signal the FCN can extract, not a shift in which
signal it relies on.

\subsection{Pairing Strategy Comparison}
The three pairing strategies (greedy first-next, Hungarian assignment, and trained
logistic scorer) are compared on held-out segmentation IoU; the resulting IoU
values are nearly identical across all models and channels regardless of
which strategy is chosen, with a maximum gap of 0.1\,pp IoU.
Once the classifier yields well-calibrated probability outputs the assignment
problem is largely trivial, and greedy first-next is selected as the default for
RF and FCN~L, while the trained scorer is selected for GBT and Hungarian for
FCN~R. Given this small gap, the specific strategy selected per model
(Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}) may partly reflect noise in the validation-set
comparison rather than a robust per-model preference; the practical conclusion is
that pairing strategy has little effect once the classifier's probability outputs
are well-calibrated.

Context probes contribute $+2.17$\,pp deployment F1, roughly three times the combined
gain of all kinematic derivative channels ($+0.68$\,pp).  The 10 raw depth samples at
$\pm$10\,ms give the RF direct access to the local depth trajectory, allowing it to
approximate velocity and acceleration implicitly from adjacent probe values; explicit
kinematic channels are therefore largely redundant, and jerk adds a negligible
$+0.04$\,pp on top. These are single-run values with no seed variance reported; the
$+2.17$\,pp probe effect is large enough to be a robust finding, but the smaller
individual kinematic-channel deltas ($+0.59$, $+0.05$, $+0.04$\,pp) are likely within
ordinary Random Forest seed-to-seed variance and should be read as suggestive rather
than precise rankings among velocity, acceleration, and jerk.

\input{chapters/figures/fig-feat-importance}

Figure~\ref{fig:feat-importance} shows how importance is distributed across channel
groups at each ablation step.  \texttt{analog\_L} --- which subsumes both window
statistics and probe values --- is the dominant group in every configuration.  When
kinematic channels are absent its share reaches 70.6\,\% (with probes) or 81.9\,\%
(window statistics only).  As kinematics are added, velocity absorbs part of that
share but \texttt{analog\_L} never falls below 31\,\%, confirming that the raw
depth trajectory carries information that derived channels cannot fully substitute.
\texttt{digital\_L} remains stable at 18--29\,\% throughout, reflecting the
independent predictive value of the OS-reported press event.

For the RF architecture, probe range and density therefore matter more than channel
engineering.  The natural next ablation is a \emph{wider-probe} study (e.g.\
$\pm$50\,ms or $\pm$100\,ms offsets) to determine whether extending the current
10\,ms probe horizon further narrows the gap to the FCN's 3\,s receptive field.

\subsection{Comparison with Static Default}
The firmware threshold optimizer is evaluated by comparing the per-user optimal
(AP, RT) configuration against the device's static default (AP\,=\,5/10, RT disabled)
across all 238 channel-records from 132 sessions and 39 participants --- the full
annotated corpus, not limited to the held-out test set. The optimizer is not trained
on any data split; it exhaustively evaluates the (AP, RT) grid for each session by
replaying the recorded analog signal through the firmware FSM simulator, so evaluating
on the full corpus does not leak test-set information used to select model
hyperparameters. For each session
and channel, the optimizer exhaustively searches a 10-level actuation-point axis
(step 12) crossed with a finer 20-level reactive-trigger axis (step 6) using the
RF model's segmented click intervals as labeled intent, applying the same fixed
production objective function as above (Section~\ref{sec:eval-setup}). The
static default is scored through the identical firmware FSM simulator to ensure a fair
comparison. Note also that this labeled intent comes from the RF's own click
segmentation, and the RF was trained on the 92\,\% of the corpus's frames used for
training and validation (Section~\ref{sec:eval-setup}); for most of the 238
channel-records evaluated here, the optimizer's target labels are therefore in-sample
outputs of a model fit on that same session's data, which should be kept in mind when
interpreting the magnitude of the reported gain.

Table~\ref{tab:optimizer} summarizes the comparison. The optimizer reduces loss by
49\,\% (mean) and increases the TP rate from 90.9\,\%
to 97.5\,\%, recovering an additional 6.6 percentage points of labeled click intent.
Because the static default (AP=5/10, RT disabled) is itself one point on the
exhaustively-searched (AP, RT) grid, $\text{loss}_{\text{optimized}} \le
\text{loss}_{\text{default}}$ holds by construction for every session --- the
optimizer cannot select a configuration that scores worse than the default under its
own objective. The Wilcoxon test statistic ($W=0$, $n=238$, $p \ll 0.001$) is
therefore not independent evidence of a real effect; it simply confirms that no
session violates this construction. These 238 channel-records are also not
independent observations --- most participants contribute both a left- and a
right-channel record across multiple sessions --- so the effective sample size for
any significance claim is smaller than 238; this reinforces reading the Wilcoxon
result as a by-construction check rather than a powered statistical test. What the
corpus-wide comparison does show empirically is the \emph{magnitude} of the
achievable gain: a mean loss reduction of 49\,\%, and a comparable relative
reduction in the median (28.4 to 15.3), indicating the improvement is broadly
distributed across the corpus rather than driven by a small number of outlying
sessions.

Unlike the RF or FCN classifiers, the optimizer is not a statistical model
with parameters fit to minimize prediction error on held-out data: it is a
bounded exhaustive search over a small discrete grid (10 AP levels
$\times$ RT on/off) that directly minimizes a deterministic, closed-form loss
against each session's own ground-truth labeled intent. With no learned
parameters at risk of overfitting noise beyond this small discrete grid,
running the comparison on the full corpus is a valid benchmark of the
achievable improvement in matching under the FSM simulator, rather than an
in-sample evaluation in the sense that applies to a trained classifier. What
this benchmark does not establish is whether a configuration fit on one
session continues to perform as well when applied prospectively to a later
session from the same participant, since within-user behavior may drift
between sessions; evaluating that would require fitting on one session and
scoring on a different session from the same participant, which we leave to
future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion-fw}).

The most practically significant result is the make-latency reduction: the static
mid-travel AP (level~5 of 10) requires the user to press well beyond the biomechanical
onset before the firmware registers the event, producing a mean make latency of
27.0\,ms.  The per-user optimized AP reduces this to 15.4\,ms --- an 11.6\,ms
reduction in make latency; we do not have a citable perceptual-threshold
reference to quantify how noticeable a change of this magnitude is to
players, so this result is best read as a measured latency reduction rather
than a confirmed perceptible responsiveness gain.

Break latency improves from a mean of 29.1\,ms to 11.2\,ms and a median of 21.5\,ms to
9.0\,ms, with no session's optimizer search selecting the maximum RT hysteresis level
(a distinct question from whether RT differentiates between users at all, addressed
separately in Section~\ref{sec:eval-user-study}): setting the detection-cost
parameter to $c_\text{det}=40$\,ms removes the optimizer's incentive to trade
break-timing accuracy for a marginal TP-rate gain by maxing out RT hysteresis, so
break latency is penalized consistently across all 132 sessions.

\section{End-to-End User Study}
\label{sec:eval-user-study}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The evaluations above quantify the pipeline's internal correctness (detection
accuracy, boundary timing) and the optimizer's theoretical gain against the FSM
simulator. Neither establishes that a per-user optimized configuration is
perceptible or beneficial to an actual player. We therefore ran a controlled,
double-blind user study to test whether the optimizer's recommendation improves
measured gameplay performance. A separate, corpus-wide trust measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-trust}) reports how participants across the full data-collection
population rate their trust in the suggested configuration, but --- since it
was collected outside the experimental/placebo split described below --- it
does not itself constitute a group comparison.

\subsection{Protocol}
This was a lab-internal pilot study conducted under Logitech's internal
data-collection and consent policy: all participants were briefed on the
study procedure and gave verbal informed consent prior to their session. The
protocol was reviewed and validated by an internal team of HCI specialists
consulted on best practices for conducting the experiment.
26 participants were recruited and randomly, evenly split into an experimental
group (calibrated configuration applied, $n=13$) and a placebo group (calibration
game played identically, but the device left at the static factory default,
AP\,=\,5/10 with RT disabled, throughout the test round, $n=13$), following a
double-blind design in which neither the participant nor the experimenter
administering the session knew the group assignment during the session. Some
of the 26 user-study participants also contributed to the 39-participant
corpus used to train the RF classifier, so the RF may have been exposed to
some study participants' own data during training; this overlap should be
kept in mind when interpreting how much of the measured gain reflects
genuine per-user generalization versus familiarity with those participants'
click patterns.
Group sizes were fixed in advance. Each session lasted
10--15 minutes and consisted of an onboarding phase (comfort settings, DPI, and a
free run to familiarize the participant with the minigames) followed by three
calibration-and-test rounds, one per task:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flicking} (1-minute calibration game, then one test round):
    fast target-acquisition task, evaluated on targets hit and shot accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Tracking} (1-minute calibration game, then one test round):
    sustained cursor-on-target task, evaluated on kill count and fraction of
    time spent on-target.
  \item \textbf{CPS} (10-second calibration game, then one 10-second test round):
    clicks-per-second task, evaluated on the raw click count over the fixed
    10-second window; because calibration and test windows are equal in
    length, the count delta is directly proportional to a click-rate delta.
\end{itemize}

Task order (Flicking, Tracking, CPS) was counterbalanced across participants
to control for ordering and fatigue effects.

To ensure uniform task difficulty across participants and reproducible comparisons
within each participant, target spawn locations and order were generated using
deterministic seeded randomization rather than independent random sequences for
each player. A single fixed seed was used across all 26 participants for each
task, ensuring calibration and test rounds are directly comparable both within and
across individuals.

\paragraph{Block-randomized target generation.}
The sequence generator uses block randomization to control target difficulty:
targets are grouped into distance ``buckets'' (five equally spaced distance values
from 200\,px to 600\,px for flicking and tracking), and within each conceptual block
of $b$ targets, each bucket distance is used exactly once, in a shuffled order.
Formally, the generator partitions the maximum expected target count (240 targets
per task) into $\lceil 240/5 \rceil = 48$ blocks of five targets each. Within every
block, the five bucket indices are shuffled using the same seed's pseudo-random stream,
then the shuffled block is appended to the sequence. Any participant who completes
$k \cdot 5$ targets in the time limit (where $k$ is an integer) encounters exactly
$k$ complete blocks, thus exactly $k$ targets at each distance, guaranteeing equal
difficulty distribution regardless of task duration or participant performance.
For participants who finish a partial block, the shortfall is at most one target
per bucket, bounding the per-bucket imbalance.

\paragraph{Controlling seed effects across per-participant calls.}
Rather than re-initializing the generator identically for both rounds (which would
require re-running the same random number stream and risk subtle drift due
to arena-size differences), the calibration round recorded the sequence of
distance, angle, and speed values for every target placement. The test round then
replayed this recorded sequence in reversed order, ensuring the exact
same targets (at the same distances and angles) reappear but traversed backward.
This reverse-replay approach: (1)~eliminates re-seeding as a source of error by
recording ground truth; (2)~provides a fresh test sequence that is geometrically
equivalent but not identical (avoiding simple memorization while preserving difficulty
balance); and (3)~preserves recorded speed samples from tracking calibration,
so evaluation rounds can reuse them rather than drawing new samples that would
pair each target's distance with an uncorrelated speed.

Within-participant, holding the target geometry constant (same positions and distances,
only in reverse order) isolates performance change as a measurement of true skill or
calibration effect. Across participants, the global seed ensures the pool of reachable
target positions and distance buckets are uniform, so inter-participant comparisons
are not biased by one participant facing systematically easier or harder geometry.

To guarantee fidelity between calibration and test sequences, all target placements
were pre-generated for all 26 participants before the study began, and each sequence
was inspected to ensure no distance-clamping or angle-fallback events occurred. This
pre-generation allowed us to verify that every target in both the calibration and
reversed test sequence was placed cleanly within the configured distance buckets
without any defensive fallbacks, eliminating any divergence between rounds due to
arena-size variation or outlier positions. The pre-generated sequences were then
loaded at runtime for each participant, ensuring exact reproducibility and uniform
difficulty across all 26 × 3 task instances.

\paragraph{Per-participant seed variance.}
Although a global seed was fixed across all participants to enable cross-participant
comparison, each participant's internal gameplay is still subject to stochasticity in
mouse movement, reaction time, and click latency — factors that are system properties,
not controlled in the protocol. The seeding of target positions is held constant
to isolate the effect of the device calibration, leaving individual behavioral
variance as an uncontrolled source of error variance, distinct from the group
assignment (calibrated vs.\ placebo) that is the actual independent variable of
interest. Each task's score is analyzed independently; the protocol does not
attempt a single unified performance metric across tasks, since each task acts
as a proxy for a distinct game context (FPS flicking, tracking-heavy shooters,
and rhythm/CPS-bound inputs) rather than a single underlying skill.

\paragraph{CPS.} Calibration and test rounds for this task are both fixed at
10 seconds, so the reported $\Delta$ is a raw click-count difference between
two equal-length windows (e.g.\ $+8$ means the experimental group registered
8 more clicks in the 10-second test round than in its own 10-second
calibration round); because the windows are equal in length, this count delta
is directly proportional to a click-rate delta. The calibrated group improved
click rate by $+10.7\,\%$ against a $-2.5\,\%$ regression in the placebo
group, a statistically significant and large effect ($p=0.035$, $d=0.92$).
The median change is more modest ($+2$ clicks vs.\ $-1$ click, i.e.\
$+3.2\,\%$ vs.\ $-2.0\,\%$ of each participant's own baseline), indicating

\paragraph{Tracking.} No metric reaches significance at $\alpha=0.05$. Two of
the three metrics trend favorably: kill count and time spent actively clicking
on-target both trend upward more for the calibrated group than for placebo,

Given the sample size, these results are hints rather than conclusive evidence.

\paragraph{Flicking.} No metric reaches significance. The calibrated group
shows a smaller improvement in targets hit than placebo ($+2$ vs.\ $+5$) and a
small decrease in accuracy relative to placebo, which we interpret cautiously:
at this sample size a $-0.6$ vs.\ $+1.5$ percentage-point accuracy difference
is plausibly noise, but it is also consistent with a plausible mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion}) in which a lower actuation point trades
click-timing margin for accuracy in fast flicking tasks. Misclicks were
reported by 3 of 13 calibrated participants on this task versus 0 of 13
placebo (Fisher's exact test, two-sided $p=0.22$); the study was not powered
to treat this as conclusive, but reporting it transparently is warranted
since it runs against the calibrated configuration.

\paragraph{Gamer-attribute interaction.} A two-way ANOVA (group $\times$ gamer
status, self-reported) tested whether self-identified gamers and non-gamers
responded differently to the intervention, for each of the six metrics in
Table~\ref{tab:user-study}: CPS clicks ($F(1,22)=1.96$, $p=0.18$), Tracking
kills ($F(1,22)=2.95$, $p=0.10$), Tracking on-target ($F(1,22)=2.47$,
$p=0.13$), Tracking clicking-on-target ($F(1,22)=0.29$, $p=0.60$), Flicking
targets hit ($F(1,22)=0.00$, $p=0.96$), and Flicking accuracy
($F(1,22)=0.02$, $p=0.88$). No interaction reaches significance on any task,
indicating the calibration's effect (or lack thereof) did not differ
measurably between the two subpopulations at this sample size.

\subsection{Recommendation Behavior}
Each task's own calibration round produced its own recommendation, applied
before that task's test round: the experimental group's device was
reconfigured to the task-specific recommended (AP, RT) after each
calibration round, so the CPS, Tracking, and Flicking test rounds each ran
under a different, task-specific configuration rather than one fixed global
value. For the placebo group, the front end never issued the corresponding
device-configuration command, so the device remained at the static default
for every task's test round regardless of what recommendation was displayed.

Independently of task performance, the optimizer produced a healthy spread of
per-user actuation-point recommendations across the 26 participants (left-channel
mean 27.5 of 120 depth levels, standard deviation 13.4, pooled across all three
tasks, $n=78$), rather than collapsing to a single value or to the static default. This is consistent with (though does
not by itself confirm) the models remaining well-behaved when driving live
per-user recommendations end-to-end, outside the offline evaluation setting of
Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt} --- spread alone does not distinguish genuine
per-user signal from noise, and the next paragraph identifies at least one
contributing artifact.

\input{chapters/figures/fig-ap-recommendations}

Figure~\ref{fig:ap-recommendations} breaks this spread down by game scenario.
Flicking and tracking calibration rounds converge to broadly similar
distributions, peaking at 24 and 36 respectively (median 24 and 36) with no
participant recommended above 48. CPS calibration is markedly more
concentrated at the lowest level (16 of 26 participants at AP 12, median 12)
yet is the only scenario with participants recommended at 60 and 72.

We attribute this high-AP tail to a behavioral artifact specific to the CPS
task rather than to noise in the optimizer itself. Once rapid trigger (RT)
engages after the switch has crossed the actuation point once, subsequent
re-clicks are driven by the release-then-cross reactive-trigger sensitivity
rather than by the actuation point, which is only consulted for the
\emph{first} click of the sequence. Participants who anticipate the CPS
prompt and pre-press before the round starts (a plausible strategy for
maximizing raw click count within the 10-second window) generate a
calibration signal in which the actuation point only ever matters for one
make event, leaving the optimizer with a single, low-information point to
fit against. This produces recommendations that are effectively
under-constrained and free to drift toward the depth extremes, rather than
genuinely indicating that a high actuation point benefits those
participants.

Recommended reactive-trigger (RT) sensitivity, by contrast, showed almost no
spread: 25 of 26 participants were assigned the lowest available level (6),
with a single participant at the next level (12). Unlike the actuation-point
recommendation, RT sensitivity did not differentiate participants in this
study, which may reflect either a genuinely uniform optimum across users or
limited sensitivity of the underlying signal at this stage of the pipeline.

\subsection{Trust in the Suggested Configuration}
\label{sec:eval-trust}
Separately from the controlled experimental/placebo comparison above,
participants were asked to rate their trust in the suggested hardware

\subsection{Study Limitations}
The study was underpowered for most secondary metrics: the 95\,\% confidence
intervals on Cohen's $d$ are wide and include zero for all five non-CPS
metrics (Table~\ref{tab:user-study}), so null results for Tracking and
Flicking should be read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect.
Applying Lehr's approximation to a representative mid-range effect size
across the six metrics ($d\approx0.75$, illustrative rather than tied to
any single metric) indicates that reaching adequately powered comparisons
(target power 0.80) would require approximately 29 participants per group
(58 total), roughly $2.2\times$ the current sample. Between-subject group assignment also left
substantial unmeasured skill-level variance uncontrolled, a single
un-warmed-up test round per task means the between-group comparison controls
for the average learning effect between calibration and test rounds, but not
for a possible group $\times$ learning-effect interaction (e.g.\ if the
calibrated configuration itself changes how much a participant improves with
practice), and gamer/non-gamer counts were not balanced in advance across
groups (8/13 vs.\ 9/13 gamers in the CPS split, for example). These
limitations are revisited in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion-lim}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Hypothesis confirmation.}
The central hypothesis of this thesis is that per-user variation in analog click
telemetry is structured enough to support automatic labeling, statistical modeling,
and parameter optimization beyond static default settings.  The results across all
three subsystems support this.  The learned classifier recovers 97.0\,\% of intended
left-channel click events, compared to only 19.9\,\% for a rule-based heuristic
that cannot detect firmware-unregistered presses.  The optimizer reduces mean make
latency by 11.6\,ms (a difference of 11.4\,ms between the two conditions' medians)
across the corpus.  As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt}, this improvement is
non-negative by construction for every session, since the static default
configuration is itself a candidate in the exhaustively-searched grid the optimizer
minimizes over; what the corpus-wide result demonstrates is the achievable
\emph{magnitude} of the gain under the FSM simulator, not yet a held-out validation
of the optimizer's generalization.
The end-to-end user study (Section~\ref{sec:eval-user-study}) provides a first,
underpowered but directionally consistent signal that this theoretical gain
carries through to measured gameplay: a statistically significant CPS improvement,
directionally favorable but inconclusive Tracking results, and a cautionary,
non-significant accuracy trade-off on Flicking.

\paragraph{The heuristic's IoU anomaly.}
An unexpected finding is that the heuristic's IoU on matched clicks (91.6\,\%) matches
the RF-L IoU (91.6\,\%) despite the RF's far higher deployment F1.  The heuristic
anchors its duration estimate on the OS digital event, which provides a reliable
proxy for the full biomechanical press-to-release window; when the OS event is
present and the heuristic correctly matches a ground-truth click, its Make and
Break boundaries track the label closely because both start from the same digital
anchor.  The models draw primarily on the analog depth signal but also weight
the OS digital event as one of several input features (18--29\,\% feature
importance, Section~\ref{sec:eval-channel-ablation}); unlike the heuristic,
however, they do not \emph{structurally anchor} their boundary estimate on
that event, so their per-matched-click IoU gains no ground on the heuristic's
digital-anchored estimate even though their overall detection rate is far
superior.  This points to a natural hybrid strategy:
use the learned classifier to detect clicks the OS cannot register, and refine the
duration estimate using the OS event when it is available.

\paragraph{Heuristic per-participant heterogeneity.}
The geometric click-intent heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:labeling}) exhibits extreme
individual variance, with per-participant F1 ranging from 0.1\,\% to 75.0\,\% on the
left channel (CV=0.79) and 0.0\,\% to 43.5\,\% on the right (CV=0.82). This spread does
not track cleanly with any single self-reported attribute (age, gaming genre, weekly
hours, or experience level) we collected, which is itself informative: it suggests the
heuristic's reliability is governed by low-level biomechanical factors ---
grip pressure (unmeasured by this system), and resting finger depth and
micro-tremor magnitude (computable from the existing depth telemetry but not
analyzed here) --- that a fixed-shape
geometric rule (peak prominence and derivative thresholds tuned on aggregate statistics)
cannot adapt to on a per-user basis. A purely geometric detector is, by construction,
a single global rule applied uniformly across the population; when the underlying
signal shape genuinely differs across individuals, no single set of thresholds can fit
all of them well, which is precisely the failure mode a learned, per-user-adaptive
classifier is designed to correct.

\paragraph{Why the RF outperforms the FCN despite its simpler architecture.}
The dilated residual FCN was expected to outperform the Random Forest by exploiting
long-range temporal context (a 3,060-frame receptive field vs.\ a hand-crafted
feature window spanning only tens of milliseconds of local context).  On the left channel the FCN falls 3.4 percentage points short of the RF
(F1 93.6\,\% vs.\ 97.0\,\%) on the single held-out test set; more critically, its
grouped cross-validation collapses to $59.7 \pm 17.4$\,pp, with one fold reaching
36.7\,\%.  On the right channel the single-test gap is 8.2 points
(83.6\,\% vs.\ 91.8\,\%) and the grouped CV mean is 76.0\,\%.  The right-channel result reveals
the underlying cause: the FCN requires sufficient labeled examples to learn
latent temporal structure from a 3-second context window, and the right-button
corpus (6,645 clicks) provides insufficient coverage.  This diagnosis is also
confounded, however, by the FCN's limited hyperparameter exploration (only
16--17 epochs, no systematic search over architecture depth, dropout, or
learning-rate schedule); the observed cross-validation collapse may partly
reflect an undertuned model rather than a purely data-driven limitation.  On
the left channel
(39,088 clicks) the FCN reaches a near-identical IoU to the RF (91.5\,\% vs.\
91.6\,\%), suggesting the two models converge to comparable boundary estimates
once data is plentiful; however, the grouped cross-validation collapse shows this
apparent parity does not reflect true cross-participant generalization.
A dedicated training-set-size ablation (Section~\ref{sec:eval-fcn-data-ablation})
partially supports this data-driven diagnosis: left-channel deployment F1 rises
steadily with corpus scale before its improvement rate begins to slow near the
current dataset size, while the right channel's deployment F1 is non-monotonic
across the same range of evaluation-split sizes, with too few ground-truth clicks
per run to establish a clean trend --- leaving open whether the right channel's
data requirement is simply larger still, or whether its underlying signal is
harder for the FCN to exploit regardless of scale.

\paragraph{Failure mode analysis.}
Fold~4 produces the lowest deployment F1 for three of the four tree-based
model/channel combinations (RF-L: 91.0\,\%, GBT-L: 91.8\,\%, GBT-R: 83.9\,\%); the
exception is RF-R, whose minimum instead falls at fold~3 (86.4\,\%).  Each grouped
cross-validation fold holds out a specific participant group, and this largely
consistent ranking suggests the participants held out in fold~4 share an atypical
click profile; a plausible but untested hypothesis is shallow resting depth or
micro-tremor-rich idle signals causing frequent false positives or missed
detections --- though the RF-R exception indicates the effect is not uniform
across every model and channel.
This is a known challenge in per-user generalization: a model trained
on other participants may not have seen enough examples of extreme profiles to learn
reliable background rejection for that subpopulation.  Active learning over the
cross-validation folds --- prioritizing annotation of participants whose profiles
diverge most from the training distribution --- is a natural direction for reducing
this variance.

\paragraph{The role of context probes.}
The dominance of context probes over engineered kinematic features
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-channel-ablation}) indicates that the production RF is
effectively operating as a probe-based trajectory pattern matcher over a
$\pm$10\,ms raw-depth window, with kinematic channels providing a modest
regularization signal on top.  This reframes the RF-versus-FCN comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-results}: both architectures ultimately draw most of their
signal from local raw-depth context rather than from explicitly derived
features, so the FCN's much larger receptive field is not yet paying for itself
at current dataset sizes.

\paragraph{Architecture, training speed, and interpretability.}
For deployment in a post-session recommendation workflow, training time is a practical
constraint.  The GBT and RF reach near-identical grouped cross-validation
generalization (mean F1 $\approx$93.3\,\% on the L channel); combined with the
FCN's cross-validation instability documented in Section~\ref{sec:eval-results}
(59.7\,\% $\pm$ 17.4\,pp vs.\ RF/GBT's $\approx$93\,\%), this rules out the FCN
as the production default but does not by itself decide between RF and GBT,
since neither shows a clear cross-validated accuracy advantage over the other.
Three further considerations are decisive. First, on the single held-out test
split RF reaches a higher deployment F1 than GBT (97.0\,\% vs.\ 93.7\,\% on the
left channel, Table~\ref{tab:deployment}); since this edge narrows to
statistical parity under grouped cross-validation, it is better read as a
tie-breaker under the standard evaluation protocol than as evidence of a
robust generalization gap. Second, training latency favors RF over the FCN:
the RF right-channel model trains in 141\,s on CPU and the
left-channel model in 23 minutes, both faster than the FCN's $\approx$44 minutes,
though the gap to the FCN (roughly $2\times$) is less decisive on its own than
the generalization gap.  The FCN's 44-minute training time is already measured
with RTX~5080 CUDA acceleration, though further speedup from software-level
optimization (mixed precision, larger batch sizes) has not been explored and
may narrow this gap; it remains acceptable for occasional offline re-calibration
but precludes on-demand per-session retraining, reinforcing the RF as the
production default until the right-channel corpus is expanded enough to justify
the FCN's architectural complexity on its own merits. This cost also scales with
corpus size: the training-set-size ablation (Section~\ref{sec:eval-fcn-data-ablation})
shows FCN-L training time growing from 226\,s to 44.9\,min across a $21.1\times$
increase in training-corpus size, so closing the right channel's data gap ---
the natural next step suggested by that ablation --- would only widen this
training-time disadvantage relative to the RF.

Third, RF and GBT differ structurally in how a prediction can be attributed
back to an input feature, which matters specifically for GBT versus RF (the
FCN's dense weight matrices are opaque under either scheme and are excluded
from this comparison). As described in
Section~\ref{sec:background-foundations}, RF averages the independent
predictions of trees grown on bootstrapped resamples~\cite{breiman_rf_2001}: each
tree's split sequence can be inspected on its own, and because the trees are
trained independently rather than on each other's residuals, the ensemble's
mean-decrease-in-impurity feature ranking is a simple, order-independent
average across trees. GBT instead fits trees sequentially, each correcting the
pseudo-residual left by the ensemble so far~\cite{friedman_gb_2001}: a single
prediction is the sum of many shallow, order-dependent corrections, so tracing
why a specific click boundary was flagged as ambiguous requires accounting for
what earlier stages had already explained away, and the resulting feature
importances can shift depending on that fitting order. For a workflow where a
developer or an on-device diagnostic routine may need to explain why the
classifier placed a boundary where it did, RF's order-independent voting is
the easier structure to inspect. This is a qualitative, architecture-level
argument rather than a measured one: no diagnostic tooling or user-facing
explanation feature was built or evaluated in this thesis, so the practical
size of this advantage remains untested.

\paragraph{Practical implications of the optimizer result.}
The static mid-travel default (AP~5/10) assumes the user can press comfortably to
50\,\% of travel before actuation, incurring a mean make latency of 27.0\,ms relative
to the user's biomechanical intent.  The optimized AP typically moves the threshold to
shallower travel, reducing make latency to 15.4\,ms and enabling the firmware to
register the user's intent almost immediately after the biomechanical onset.  As
noted in Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt}, we do not have a citable perceptual-threshold
reference for this specific context, so the 11.6\,ms reduction is reported here
as a measured latency improvement rather than a confirmed perceptible gain.
Setting the detection-cost parameter to $c_\text{det}=40$\,ms also eliminates
degenerate maximum-RT-hysteresis selections, so break latency is penalized
consistently across all 132 sessions without a measurable cost to TP rate.

\paragraph{From theoretical to measured gains.}
The user study (Section~\ref{sec:eval-user-study}) provides the first evidence
that the optimizer's simulator-measured latency reduction translates into
observable gameplay change, at least for CPS: the calibrated group's $+10.7\,\%$
click-rate gain against a placebo regression ($p=0.035$, $d=0.92$) is consistent
with the make-latency improvement reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval-opt}, since a
lower actuation point directly shortens the interval between successive presses
in a rapid-click task.
The Tracking results trend in the same direction without
reaching significance, plausibly because tracking performance depends more on
mouse-movement precision than on click latency, diluting the effect of a
click-timing-only intervention. The Flicking result is the one point of tension
with the theoretical model: a lower actuation point that improves make latency
could simultaneously reduce the physical travel margin available before an
unintended actuation, which would manifest as the observed (non-significant)
accuracy decrease and elevated misclick count (3/13 vs.\ 0/13) in a fast,
high-force flicking motion. This suggests the optimizer's objective function,
which currently scores actuation timing in isolation, may need to account for
mouse-movement dynamics to avoid trading click-timing gains for accuracy losses
in flick-dominant contexts. Beyond reshaping the objective itself, the same
trajectory signal could inform \emph{how} a recommended change is delivered:
favoring the sensitivity that least disrupts the user's existing motor habits,
or ramping the actuation point toward the target value progressively across
sessions rather than applying it as a single abrupt change --- concrete
directions discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion-fw}.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The chapter's findings are synthesized at the document level in the concluding
chapter (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion-lim} in particular carries forward two open
methodological items from this discussion: the optimizer's cross-session
robustness, i.e.\ whether a configuration fit on one session still performs
well on a later session from the same participant, remains untested, and the
FCN's cross-validation collapse is confounded with its limited hyperparameter
search). Neither caveat overturns the central finding, but together they
temper how strongly it should be read pending the follow-up work outlined
above.
